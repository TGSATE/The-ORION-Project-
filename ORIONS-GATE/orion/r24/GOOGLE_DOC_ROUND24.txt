In quantum computing, an 8-dimensional qudit replaces three qubits, since $8=2^3$. The operators $W_{s,t}$ provide the generalized Pauli gates. They are used to compile a circuit, formulate an error-correcting code for the qudit, and track how an error propagates through discrete phase space. The Heisenberg group—of order 512—classifies these errors, while $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})$—of order 384—determines which transformations leave the code invariant. $H=\sum_{s,t}c_{s,t}W_{s,t}$, where $c_{s,t}=\frac{1}{8}\operatorname{Tr}(W_{s,t}^\dagger H)$. The coefficients $c_{s,t}$ represent transition frequencies and couplings; they are tuned via microwave or laser pulses applied to an ion, a superconducting circuit, or a multi-level nuclear spin. The clock (Z) and shift (X) operators are the two generators that the experimental setup must be capable of applying. In quantum information, the 64-state phase space serves as a lookup table. A measurement in a basis shifted by $W_{s,t}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$-valued measurement. This approach allows for the construction of key distribution protocols, state tomography, and entanglement tests for pairs of qudits. The case $d=2$ recovers the Pauli matrices and standard qubit protocols; the case $d=8$ follows the same construction but involves more levels.
