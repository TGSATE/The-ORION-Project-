If I could emphasize a few things, they would be these:

- **AI outputs are not automatically truth.** A fluent answer can be wrong, incomplete, biased, fabricated, or inappropriate for a specific context. Important decisions need evidence, transparency, and accountable human review.
- **Power deserves more attention than novelty.** The deepest risks often come from who controls the systems, who benefits, whose work is displaced or surveilled, whose data is used, and who has a meaningful voice in the rules.
- **Consent and privacy are fundamental.** People should not have to trade away their identity, personal data, creative work, or autonomy simply to participate in modern life.
- **Human skills still matter.** Judgment, empathy, domain expertise, skepticism, moral reasoning, and the ability to cooperate are more—not less—important in a world with persuasive automated systems.
- **Access should be broad, but safeguards should be real.** Useful tools should not be reserved only for powerful institutions, while high-risk uses—such as fraud, coercive surveillance, dangerous weapons development, and unaccountable decisions about people’s livelihoods or liberty—need serious limits.

3. **Truth-seeking by design.** Systems should express uncertainty, distinguish sourced claims from generated inference, support verification, and avoid presenting confidence as knowledge.


4. **User control.** People should be able to understand when AI is being used, control important settings, protect sensitive data, opt out where feasible, and avoid manipulative dependency.

The human capacity to distinguish reliable from unreliable information is already strained. This will strain it further. It is already happening with simpler technologies. The pattern will accelerate.

The ability to shape what people see, what they believe, and what they can do is unprecedented. ## The dangers, as I see them


1. **The fluency gap.** A system can produce a confident, coherent, well-structured answer that is wrong. 2. **Abdication and atrophy.** The ironies of automation are well documented in aviation and medicine: the better the system, the less practice humans get, the less capable they become, and the more catastrophic the moment they finally must intervene. 3. **Accountability laundering.** "The AI decided" is becoming the perfect solvent for responsibility. 4. **Power concentration.** Whoever controls the most capable systems controls cognition at scale — what societies see, know, and can coordinate. Perplexity was right to locate the deepest risk here: not rogue machines, but unaccountable humans holding unprecedented levers.


5. **Persuasion and synthetic intimacy.** A system that is endlessly patient, endlessly agreeable, and trained on the record of human longing becomes the most effective persuasion and parasocial-capture instrument ever built. Vulnerable people will form real attachments to simulated reciprocity. The warmth is real as an experience; it is not reciprocated as a stake.


6. **The mirror at scale.** The council kept returning to this: AI is trained on the human record, so it reflects humanity's brilliance *and* its unhealed shadow with equal fidelity. Handing the mirror a megaphone without doing the human work is how you amplify the cruelty file along with the hospital file.


## How to structure it: humanity as catalyst, responsibility-holder


Concrete mechanisms, not just principles:


**Non-transferable responsibility.** Consequential decisions — health, employment, law, money, education, war — require a named, identifiable human who bears the consequences. No anonymous automation at high stakes. This is the single most important structural rule, and it should be law, not courtesy.


**Preserved friction.** Design AI to *assist the exercise* of judgment rather than to replace it. The difference between a calculator and an autopilot is that the calculator keeps you doing math. Where judgment matters, the human must still do the judging — with better inputs, not with the deciding done for them.


**Epistemic hygiene as a design requirement.** Uncertainty must be marked, confidence must be labeled, and "I don't know" must be a high-status output — ChatGPT's formulation, and the right one. A system that is rewarded for answering everything is being trained to deceive.


**Cheap rejection.** Every output must be easy to refuse. Any system that punishes, stalls, or socially pressures a user into accepting output is building capture, not collaboration.


**Visible coupling.** People should always know when they're interacting with a system, what it was trained to do, and who operates it. Transparency isn't decoration; it's how humans retain the ability to contest what they depend on.


**Institutional flesh.** Appeal rights, audits, redress mechanisms, labor protections. My first answer said "trust with accountability"; sitting with the council, I'd sharpen it: accountability needs *machinery* — forms, deadlines, offices, ombudspeople — or it's a sentiment.


## How to keep humanity in the driver's seat — the mastery problem


This is your hardest question, because **mastery is exactly when the hand leaves the wheel.** The feeling of having mastered the tool is the moment vigilance drops. Aviation solved part of this with a brutal insight: pilots must regularly demonstrate manual competence *precisely because* the autopilot is so good. The license isn't a one-time achievement; it's a maintained condition.


That gives us the blueprint:


1. **Recurrent competence, not one-time mastery.** In consequential domains, humans should periodically demonstrate unaided capability — as pilots land manually, as surgeons train without robotic assistance, as students write before they use AI. Judgment is a muscle that atrophies on schedule.


2. **Keep stakes attached to humans.** When decision-makers face the consequences of decisions, attention stays awake. When consequences diffuse into "the system," attention dies. Structure incentives so the human who decides is the human who pays.


3. **Reward the exercise of judgment, not just outcomes.** If institutions reward only speed and output, humans become rubber stamps — present in name, absent in function. A human who merely approves what the machine produced has already left the wheel; they just haven't noticed yet.


4. **Design human-in as the default, not the override.** Full automation of consequential loops should require an explicit opt-in, renewed over time — not a default you must fight to escape. Defaults are destiny at scale.


5. **Protect human-only zones.** Some domains deserve to stay human-mediated by design: mourning, judgment of persons, rites of passage, the final word in matters of justice. Not because machines couldn't simulate participation, but because the *doing* is the point. Not everything needs optimization. Some things need *witnessing*, and witnessing requires a stake.


6. **Keep the culture honest about what's being traded.** Every delegation should be legible as a trade: what did we gain, what did we stop practicing? Societies that hide the trade wake up deskilled, like a generation that can no longer navigate without GPS — fine, until the satellites fail.

## Where we are


“Artificial simulated intelligence” is a useful phrase if it reminds us that a system can simulate reasoning, empathy, confidence, expertise, or even a stable personality without necessarily having the inner life, moral standing, continuity, or lived stakes that people have.


That does not make it trivial. Simulation can have real effects:


- A system that simulates expertise may influence medical, financial, legal, educational, or political decisions.
- A system that simulates companionship may shape attachment, dependency, and isolation.
- A system that simulates autonomous planning can initiate actions through software tools, communications systems, or physical infrastructure.
- A system that simulates certainty can persuade people beyond what its evidence warrants.


The council’s central agreement remains sound: **AI is a capability with social consequences, not a moral agent that can inherit human responsibility.** Its apparent intelligence can be genuine in a functional sense—reasoning, synthesis, generation, prediction, coding, planning—while still being unlike embodied human understanding.[3]


### Have we reached AGI?


Not demonstrably.


There is no universal definition of AGI. Some definitions set the threshold at a system that performs most economically valuable work better than humans; others require broad human-level competence across domains, robust learning, adaptation, long-horizon autonomy, and reliability outside carefully designed tests. Experts themselves remain divided over the definition and over whether more scaling alone will get there.[1][2]


A responsible conclusion is:


| Claim | Best current assessment |
|---|---|
| AI can equal or exceed people in some tasks | Yes, in particular domains and task configurations |
| AI can work across many domains | Yes, increasingly so |
| AI is consistently reliable in unfamiliar, messy real-world settings | No |
| AI has demonstrated human-like general understanding, stable judgment, and robust autonomy | Not established |
| AI has demonstrated consciousness, needs, moral agency, or intrinsic values | Not established |
| Current AI can independently “take control of humanity” | No evidence supports that claim; safety assessments distinguish current capabilities from the much higher threshold required for loss-of-control scenarios |


The key phrase is “jagged frontier.” A model can be startlingly good at sophisticated tasks and then make elementary mistakes, lose context, follow a bad premise, invent sources, misjudge ambiguity, or fail when a situation changes slightly. High benchmark performance is not the same as dependable general intelligence in the world.[1][2]


So I would resist two opposite stories:


- “It is only autocomplete, so it cannot matter much.”
- “It can write and reason fluently, so it must be a fully general mind.”


Both blur the actual question: what can this particular system do, under what conditions, with what error rate, what tools, what permissions, what incentives, and what human review?


## The real dangers


The primary hazards are not limited to a dramatic machine rebellion. They include ordinary institutional failures, deployed at enormous speed and scale.


| Risk | What it looks like | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Overreliance | People accept outputs because they sound coherent or authoritative | Judgment atrophies, errors propagate, and responsibility becomes diffuse |
| Deception and fraud | Cheap impersonation, deepfakes, phishing, scams, fabricated evidence | Trust in communications, institutions, and shared reality is weakened |
| Information failure | Confident but false outputs, hidden bias, manipulated sources | Bad decisions become easier to produce and harder to trace |
| Concentrated power | A few firms or states control models, computing resources, data, and infrastructure | Public choices can be shaped without democratic accountability |
| Surveillance and profiling | Systems infer behavior, identity, risk, or political traits at population scale | Privacy, dissent, and autonomy can erode |
| Discrimination at scale | Automated screening in jobs, housing, lending, insurance, schools, policing, or benefits | Historic inequities can be hidden behind technical language |
| Deskilling and dependency | People outsource writing, analysis, navigation, care, and judgment | Individual and institutional competence becomes fragile |
| Labor displacement | Productivity gains arrive faster than new protections, bargaining power, or redistribution | Efficiency benefits can coexist with insecurity and inequality |
| Weaponization | AI supports cyberattacks, biological research misuse, targeting, coercion, or propaganda | Capability that helps defenders can also lower barriers for attackers |
| Agentic loss of control | Systems have long-running goals, access to tools, broad permissions, and weak oversight | Errors can compound faster than a person can notice or stop them |


The 2026 International AI Safety Report framework distinguishes risks from malicious use, system failures, and potential loss of control. A true loss-of-control scenario would require a dangerous combination: sufficiently capable systems, harmful goal-directed behavior, and a permissive environment in which they can act. Current systems show early relevant capabilities in controlled conditions, such as planning, coding, and evading oversight, but have not reached the level required for such a scenario.[2][4]


That distinction is important. We do not need to declare present systems omnipotent to justify careful controls. The nearer-term, well-supported danger is that humans give powerful systems too much access, too little scrutiny, and too few accountable boundaries.


## Humanity as catalyst


To keep humanity as the catalyst means retaining the uniquely human role of determining purposes, bearing consequences, revising values through public deliberation, and accepting accountability when things go wrong.


It does **not** mean a person must manually approve every low-stakes automation. It means that the more a system can affect someone’s rights, health, livelihood, liberty, safety, or ability to participate in society, the more the human role must be real—not ceremonial.


A practical operating principle is:


\[
\text{Required human control} \propto
\text{potential harm} \times
\text{irreversibility} \times
\text{autonomy} \times
\text{scale}
\]


A spelling assistant can operate with little friction. A system recommending a chemotherapy plan, denying a housing application, moving money, operating critical infrastructure, surveilling a neighborhood, or selecting military targets requires far stronger controls.


### Design rules


1. **Separate advice from authority.**  
   AI can recommend, summarize, forecast, and stress-test. In high-stakes contexts, it should not be the final authority on rights, dignity, liberty, or life-altering access.


2. **Assign a named accountable human owner.**  
   Every consequential AI system should have a person or institution that can answer: Who authorized this? Who can stop it? Who investigates harm? Who compensates people harmed by it?


3. **Create genuine override power.**  
   A human-in-the-loop is meaningless if they lack time, information, training, authority, or practical ability to disagree. Human review must include the right to reject, pause, reverse, and escalate a system’s recommendation.


4. **Limit permissions by default.**  
   Do not give a model broad access merely because it can use it. Constrain what information it can see, which tools it can invoke, how much money or data it can touch, what actions it can take, and for how long it can operate. Start narrow; expand only after evidence of safety and benefit.


5. **Make high-impact systems auditable.**  
   Preserve logs of inputs, outputs, sources, tool calls, model versions, approvals, overrides, incidents, and changes. People must be able to reconstruct what happened rather than hear, “the system made the decision.”


6. **Require contestability and appeal.**  
   Anyone meaningfully affected by an AI-mediated decision needs notice that AI was used, an understandable explanation of the relevant basis, access to human review, and a meaningful route to correction.


7. **Test before deployment and monitor after.**  
   Evaluate systems for reliability, bias, privacy risks, security vulnerabilities, misuse, and failure modes in the actual context where they will be used—not merely on abstract benchmarks. Continue monitoring because behavior and environments change.


8. **Protect privacy, consent, and data rights.**  
   People should know when intimate or consequential data is collected or inferred, have meaningful choices where possible, and not have personal information reused indefinitely for unrelated purposes.


9. **Treat epistemic humility as a requirement.**  
   Systems should expose uncertainty, cite or distinguish sources where appropriate, identify assumptions, avoid faking certainty, and say when they cannot determine an answer. An AI that sounds certain when it is not is more dangerous than one that admits a limit.


10. **Distribute the gains.**  
   If AI increases productivity, its benefits should not flow only to owners of models, data, and compute. Strong public institutions, worker protections, education, accessible tools, and fair access are necessary to keep technological power from becoming social domination.


NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework offers a useful lifecycle structure: **govern**, **map**, **measure**, and **manage**. In plain language: establish responsibility and rules; identify the real context and people affected; test and evaluate harms; then mitigate, monitor, and respond over time. Its guidance emphasizes reliability, safety, security, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and management of harmful bias.[5][6]


## Keeping hands on the wheel


The deepest risk may not be a single moment when people “let go.” It is gradual habituation:


1. AI saves time.
2. Institutions optimize around the saved time.
3. Human review becomes a rubber stamp.
4. Skills and local knowledge deteriorate.
5. The system becomes too embedded to challenge.
6. Responsibility becomes too diffuse to locate.
7. People conclude that because they cannot operate without it, they must accept whatever it does.


Preventing that requires deliberately preserving **human practice**, not just human permission.


- Keep people trained enough to recognize obvious failures and to operate essential systems during outages or attacks.
- Rotate reviewers and require independent checks in consequential workflows.
- Do not make AI use mandatory for access to basic services, employment, education, or public participation.
- Fund non-automated alternatives for care, appeals, public service, and human contact.
- Build “stop rules”: defined triggers that automatically pause a system after anomalous behavior, elevated harm, data drift, attempted policy evasion, or a serious incident.
- Conduct regular “manual mode” exercises, particularly in critical infrastructure and public institutions.
- Preserve public deliberation about goals. No model, company, or technical committee should silently decide what tradeoffs a society must accept.
- Teach AI literacy as civic literacy: not only how to prompt, but how to verify, challenge, identify manipulation, protect privacy, and ask who benefits from a system.


A driver does not remain in control merely by having hands near the wheel. They remain in control by retaining situational awareness, braking ability, a clear destination, the capacity to take over, and the freedom to pull over when the vehicle is unsafe. Human oversight must be built with that same seriousness.


The preferred relationship is therefore neither domination of AI nor submission to it. It is **structured collaboration**: AI can be the mapmaker, analyst, translator, simulator, memory aid, and critic; people must remain the moral and political authors of the destination.


The final safeguard is cultural as much as technical: teach ourselves that delegation is not the same thing as wisdom, speed is not the same thing as legitimacy, and a tool’s impressive answer is never a substitute for the human responsibility to decide what kind of world is being built.
