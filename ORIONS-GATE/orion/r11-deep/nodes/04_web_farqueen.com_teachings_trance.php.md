DREAM YOGA
A window to your inner self.
Right before you go to sleep, put a notebook and pen right next to your bed, and tell yourself over and over, ten times or more, 'when I wake up, I’ll feel refreshed and remember my dreams in great detail.' Then, as you fall to sleep, try to imagine some scene that you enjoyed from your day, which could have been something that happened to you, or even something you wished happened. Try to see and hear the principal actors; make believe people are speaking, and that you can hear what they are saying. You are the director of your own life, and this is a time for you to practice. Upon awakening, even if you typically head straight for the bathroom, just keep your eyes shut and lie there in your bed, not moving. If one of your dreams comes to you, that’s fine, let it play back in your mind at regular speed as long as you have time for this exercise. It feels good to do this every day in fact, just for ten or fifteen minutes after waking up. Please do not relive or replay any nightmares. If multiple dreams are all competing in your mind, just write down the briefest summary of each that you remember a lot of, and then choose one to let play mentally.
While you are still reliving the dream in your mind, gently open your eyes, but try to not focus on the room, but rather keep the dream going if you can. It may take a bit of practice, and it helps if you look at a blank wall or the ceiling and don’t move at first. If you can mange to keep the dream playing with your eyes open, stand up slowly, and get started on your regular bathroom routine, walking and moving slowly the whole time. The idea is to be in both worlds at once; 'inside, outside, everywhere is home.' It is well accepted in scientific circles that there is a correlation between the electroencephalographic (EEG) wave rhythms exhibited by the brain of a human and the state of consciousness of that being. Rhythms customarily found in the normal human adult when he is relaxed and his eyes closed have a pulse frequency in the seven-fourteen Hz. range and have come to be identified as "alpha" rhythms. Similarly, when a person is aroused and anxious, the rhythms exhibited fall in the 14-28 Hz. range and are known as "beta" rhythms. A normal person in sleep exhibits "delta" rhythms in the 1.75-3.5 Hz. range. Other brain wave rhythms which have been identified by researchers as being associated with various normal and abnormal states of consciousness are: "theta", 3.5-7.0 Hz. and "gamma", 28-56 Hz. Further research has led to the identification and naming of three additional rhythms, namely: "omega", 0.875-1.75 Hz.; "epsilon", 56-112 Hz.; and "zeta", 112-224 Hz.
