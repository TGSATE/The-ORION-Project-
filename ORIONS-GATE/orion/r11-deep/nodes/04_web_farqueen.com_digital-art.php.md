Image Specification for AI Graphics
subject - specifying the core identity, character, or entity present in the scene.
spatial coordinate - defining the exact placement, arrangement, and layout of objects within the frame.
scale and proportion - adjusting relative sizes to establish dominance, perspective, and monumental scale.
temporal moment - anchoring the action to a specific phase of unfolding time, such as mid-action or stillness.
architectural typology - detailing the structural engineering style, historical period, and material composition of buildings.
atmospheric condition - engineering meteorological states, storm density, lighting direction, and cloud formations.
surface texture - calibrating tactile qualities like marble grain, polished metal, ragged fabric, or rough stone.
color palette - selecting chromatic harmonies, dominant tones, contrasts, and emotional color grading.
illumination source - defining primary and secondary light vectors, shadows, highlights, and ambient glow.
emotional register - encoding internal psychological states, terror, awe, resolve, or ecstasy into facial expressions and posture.
camera framing - choosing the exact vantage point, height, and boundary containment of the scene.
focal length - simulating optical characteristics like wide -angle distortion or telephoto compression.
depth of field - directing visual attention by controlling what is sharply focused versus blurred in background and foreground.
historical epoch - aligning stylistic details with a specific era, whether ancient, medieval, renaissance, or futuristic.
material authenticity - ensuring physical substances reflect light, wear, and structural integrity accurately.
narrative tension - structuring the scene to maximize the conflict or dynamic pull between opposing elements.
stylistic rendering - determining whether the output mimics classical painting, high -end
photography, or surrealism.
clothing and drapery - specifying the cut, drape, fabric weight, and ornamentation of garments.
ornamental iconography - placing symbolic crowns, seals, jewels, or markers of status and office.
kinetic energy - capturing motion vectors, flying debris, falling water, or rushing wind currents.
topographical layout - defining geographical features, cliffs, coastlines, valleys, or elevated plateaus.
industrial design - detailing mechanical components like gears, struts, rivets, and propulsion mechanisms.
atmospheric perspective - managing the haziness and color shift of objects viewed at great distances.
gestural anatomy - posing hands, fingers, and body mechanics to communicate precise intent or vulnerability.
compositional balance - distributing visual weight to create stability, asymmetry, or deliberate instability.
shadow density - controlling the depth of shadows from soft ambient occlusion to harsh, high -contrast gloom.
reflectivity - setting how surfaces catch mirror images, wet sheen, or metallic glare.
scale contrast - juxtaposing tiny human elements against massive structures or natural forces to evoke the sublime.
cultural design motif - weaving specific architectural and artistic patterns characteristic of a fictional or historical civilization.
occlusion and layering - managing overlapping elements to build complex, deep spatial environments.
translucency - defining how light passes through materials like stained glass, water, or gossamer fabric.
weather particle - distributing wind -blown dust, rain spray, ash, or debris across the atmosphere.
architectural permanence - contrasting pristine standing monuments with fractured ruins or structural decay.
vantage height - placing the viewer above, at eye level with, or looking up at the subject to dictate power dynamics.
chromatic saturation - modulating color intensity from desaturated, bleak tones to vibrant, saturated hues.
edge sharpness - dictating whether boundaries are crisp and well -defined or soft and atmospheric.
structural rhythm - repeating architectural motifs like columns, spires, or arches to create visual cadence.
clothing damage - showing wear, tears, stains, or battle damage to reflect recent narrative events.
cinematic mood - tuning the visual atmosphere to match specific cinematic genres like epic, thriller, or drama.
foreground framing - using elements right at the camera boundary to pull the viewer deeper into the space.
scale transition - ensuring smooth optical progression from minute background details to massive foreground subjects.
lighting temperature - shifting color tones between cold blue moonlight and warm golden hour radiance.
mechanical scale - ensuring vehicles like airships or machinery feel massive through structural detailing and rivets.
symbolic alignment - matching background architecture and weather directly to the emotional state of the central figure.
surface weathering - adding patina, oxidation, soot, or water stains to establish age and environmental exposure.
gaze direction - aiming the subject's eyes to create an active psychological connection with an off -camera threat.
proportional geometry - applying classical grids, golden ratios, or sacred geometry to compositions.
background separation - keeping the main subject distinct from complex environmental backdrops without flat cutouts.
environmental scale - accurately rendering the sheer magnitude of massive phenomena like 500 -meter waves.
resolution specification - defining the absolute density of structural information packed into every sector of the frame.
