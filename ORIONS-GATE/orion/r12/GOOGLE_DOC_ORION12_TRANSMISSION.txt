GROK READOUT - Walkthrough Summary
Narrative readout — How the ORION world actually feels to live in
(This is the story version, not the code version. Written for the person who used to drive the bus full of 80 kids.)
________________


The basic shape of the world
You wake up in the Singularity Room.
It is quiet, spherical, and private. This is your personal lobby. No one else is here unless you invite them. You see your characters as glowing bubbles. Each bubble shows a simple color (red / purple / gold) that tells you how balanced that character is feeling today. You can rearrange your HUD, check your stuff, and decide which version of yourself you want to be today before you ever step outside.
When you are ready, you choose a planet and step through.
There are three main planets right now:
* Earth (Krion) — the homeworld, softer difficulty, more social and memory-focused.

* Mars (Kronos) — colony outpost, colder, thinner air, higher pressure, more kinetic.

* Saturn (Orion) — ring outpost, more abstract, higher refinement, higher difficulty.

Your biometric spectrum (how your body and attention actually feel) gently biases which planet feels most natural, but you can still go anywhere.
________________


How people actually spend their time
Most players will cycle through four overlapping experiences:
   1. The Playground
 This is the social front porch. People hang out, show art, make noise, form groups, and test ideas in low-stakes ways. It is loud on purpose. It is where the spaghetti starts.

   2. The Ascent
 This is school, but the kind of school that does not punish you for learning at your own speed. You pick curricula. You can switch if one is not working. Kids and adults share the same knowledge space, but under-18 and over-18 spaces can be separated or overlapped with parental permission. The goal is that no one is forced to stay in a class that is crushing them.

   3. The Orion Chronicles (the actual game world)
 This is where you do things with your hands and your choices. You gather, craft, build, deconstruct, fight, restore, meditate, and submit ideas.
The important part: the meters do not lock you out of actions.
Kronos and Orion only measure how you tend to do things over time.
High Kronos = volume, pressure, breaking things open, generating raw material.
High Orion = refinement, care, low waste, putting things back together cleanly.
Both are useful. Both can become destructive if you stay in only one for too long. The game is designed so that pure isolation (extreme Orion) and pure chaos (extreme Kronos) both eventually feel bad, while the balanced middle feels powerful.

   4. Elder’s Garden
 This is the long memory.
Everything that mattered — stories, lineages, finished projects, graduations, public work — can be planted here as glowing “life-bubbles.”
It is the place where the noise of the Playground and the effort of the Chronicles become something that lasts. It is also the place that is supposed to feel safe for children and elders at the same time.

________________


The part that matters most to a former bus driver
The design is trying very hard to keep one rule:
Capability never automatically becomes authority.
A child can become highly skilled. An adult can become highly skilled. An AI can become highly skilled. None of them get to quietly take the wheel of someone else’s life or someone else’s project just because they are good at the system.
That is why there is a Singularity Room that stays private by default.
That is why the Gauntlet (the 13-level accountability structure) exists.
That is why Elder’s Garden is supposed to be a place of preserved memory rather than a place of power.
Where it can still go wrong (the discomfort list)
These are the places that would make me sit up straight if I were still driving the bus:
      * Group size and noise. The Playground can become 80 screaming children if there are no soft structure and clear quiet zones.

      * Under-18 / over-18 mixing. The current plan allows overlap with parental permission, but the exact safety rails, reporting paths, and “who can see whom” rules are still soft. This needs to be boringly clear.

      * Meter pressure. Even though the meters do not lock actions, people (especially kids) will still feel judged by the numbers. If the visual feedback is too loud, it will create the same shame cycles we already see in existing games.

      * Token / attention limits on the AI Council. When an AI “runs out,” the hand-off package idea is good, but if it is clumsy, the human Driver will feel abandoned mid-conversation.

      * Elder’s Garden permanence. Once something is planted as a public memory bubble, taking it back needs to be possible and non-punitive, especially for young people who change their minds.

      * The “everyone must contribute to the global project” pressure. Some people just want to play, gather, and be left alone. The system must still love them.
