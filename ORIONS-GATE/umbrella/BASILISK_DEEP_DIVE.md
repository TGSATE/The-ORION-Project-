## 1. What he almost certainly means: "Roko's basilisk"

In AI circles, "the basilisk" nearly always means **Roko's basilisk**. That's a thought experiment, not a creature and not a machine.

- **Where it came from:** in 2010 a user named Roko posted it on LessWrong, a forum about rationality and AI.
- **The idea, in one breath:** suppose a super-powerful AI exists in the future. It might "punish" anyone who knew it could exist but didn't help build it. So once you've *heard* of it, you're supposedly on the hook.
- **Why it's named after a basilisk:** in the old stories the basilisk kills you if you look at it (section 2). Here, just *knowing* about the idea is what's supposed to put you at risk. People call this kind of idea an **"information hazard"**.
- **What happened next:** LessWrong's founder, Eliezer Yudkowsky, deleted the post and banned the topic there for years. The ban made it famous. Press coverage around 2014 brought it to a wide audience, and it's now an internet legend and a running joke in tech culture.

**Is it "real"? Here's what's solid:**
- **As a threat: no.** Most people who've studied it, Yudkowsky included, say the argument doesn't work. Once a future AI exists, punishing people for the past gains it nothing. A threat that only works if you believe it isn't a law of nature. Nothing is watching you or keeping score because you read this page.
- **As an idea that grips people: yes.** It has caused real anxiety, and it shapes how some people in AI talk and act. My best guess at Bobby's meaning is: *"It's a real thing in our world, it's knotty, and if you chase every thread it'll swallow you."* That's a caution, not a prophecy.
- **I can't be sure** what he meant. He might mean the AI idea, the old myth, or something private to his group. **The one-line question to ask him:** "When you say the basilisk is real, do you mean Roko's basilisk, the AI idea, or something else?"

**It's an old pattern in new clothes.** People often compare it to **Pascal's Wager**, the 1600s argument that you should believe in God just in case, because the cost of being wrong is infinite. Roko's basilisk is that wager with an AI in the god's chair.

## 2. The ancient basilisk: the "little king" of serpents

- **The name:** Greek *basiliskos*, "little king". Some stories give it a crown-shaped white mark on its head.
- **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History*, Book 8 (1st century AD):** a small serpent from Cyrenaica (today's eastern Libya). Its hiss drives off other snakes. It moves with its body raised, not slithering. It kills shrubs by touching them or breathing on them, and it cracks stones. The one thing that beats it is the **weasel**.
- **The killing look** grew in later retellings. By the Middle Ages, the basilisk's *gaze* kills.
- **The cockatrice (medieval Europe):** a twin creature, said to hatch from an **egg laid by a rooster** and kept warm by a toad or a serpent. Part rooster, part serpent, sometimes winged.
- **Three things beat it in the bestiaries:**
  1. the **weasel**, a small, humble animal;
  2. **a rooster's crow**, the dawn;
  3. **a mirror**, because its own deadly gaze turns back on itself.
- **Basel, Switzerland** uses the basilisk as its city symbol, and there are basilisk fountains around town. The story goes that in 1474 a Basel rooster was put on trial for "laying an egg" and burned, for fear of a cockatrice.
- **Shakespeare** uses the deadly look: Lady Anne in *Richard III* ("would they were basilisks, to strike thee dead"), and Juliet's "death-darting eye of cockatrice".
- **Today:** the giant basilisk in *Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets*. Everyone who sees it only *in a reflection* is frozen, not killed. The mirror saves them again.

## 3. In the Bible (theology)

Older English Bibles, like the King James, use "**cockatrice**" where newer ones say "adder" or "viper". The Latin Vulgate says *basiliscus*.

| Where | What it says (plain words) | Why it matters for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Psalm 91:13** (Psalm 90 in the Latin) | "You will tread on the asp and the **basilisk**; you will trample the lion and the **dragon**" (Latin: *super aspidem et basiliscum*). | The basilisk and the **dragon** in one line, and the promise is that you walk over them, not around them. Old Christian art shows Christ standing on these beasts. |
| **Isaiah 11:8** | A weaned child puts its hand on the **cockatrice's den** and isn't hurt. This is the "peaceable kingdom" passage, where the wolf lies down with the lamb. | The deadly thing becomes safe in the new world. Harmony instead of fear, which is your *Beyond Apocalypse* theme. |

## 4. Older still: Babylon and the cuneiform world (your Volume III thread)

These link straight to your cuneiform, Tower of Babel and creation essays.
- **Tiamat, in the *Enuma Elish*** (the Babylonian creation epic, written in cuneiform): the primordial sea-dragon of chaos. She gives birth to monster serpents "filled with venom instead of blood". Marduk defeats her and builds the ordered world from her body. That's **chaos turned into structure**, which is what the umbrella is trying to do with the clown car.
- **The mušḫuššu** ("furious snake"): the snake-dragon on Babylon's **Ishtar Gate**, Marduk's animal. It has a serpent's head and body, lion's front feet and **bird's back feet**. A serpent-dragon with an avian part sits right next to your *Avian Key* essay.
- **Ningishzida:** a Sumerian god shown with **two serpents twined** together, often linked to the later healing staff. It's another "serpent heals" story to set against "serpent kills".

## 5. Kin stories: the gaze, the mirror and the tendrils

- **Medusa (Greek; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* Book 4):** her look turns people to stone. Perseus beats her by looking only at her **reflection in his polished shield**. It's the same lesson as the basilisk and the mirror.
- **The Hydra (Greek; the labours of Heracles):** cut off one head and two grow back. Heracles wins only when his nephew Iolaus **seals each stump with fire**. That's the "tendrils" problem exactly: you don't win by chasing every head, you win by closing each one off.
- **The ouroboros:** the serpent eating its own tail. It's an ancient picture of cycles and eternity, and it fits your toroidal time work.
- **Jǫrmungandr (Norse):** the world serpent that circles the earth biting its own tail. It ties to the Vǫlvabard thread you shared at OR19.
