## 3. How bad reactions to medications and unusual reactions to THC are connected 🔬

The short version: **the same body systems that handle medications also handle THC.** If those systems work differently in you, it's not surprising that you're unusual on **both**. Here are the main ways that happens, from best established to more speculative.

### 3a. Your liver's "processing enzymes" (best established)

- Most medications, **and THC and CBD**, are broken down by one family of liver enzymes, called **cytochrome P450** (CYP for short).
- **People inherit different versions of these enzymes.** Some people break a drug down **very slowly** ("poor metabolisers"). Others do it **very fast** ("ultrarapid metabolisers"). The field that studies this is called **pharmacogenomics**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - A **slow** processor can build up **too much** of a drug, so a normal dose acts like an overdose. That can include side effects such as **seizures**, and the drug can take an unusually long time to leave.
  - Some drugs only work after the liver **converts** them into an active form. A **fast** converter gets a **sudden, stronger** dose of the active form; a slow one gets almost none.
  - Either way, you get effects that are very different from "the label".
- **THC** goes through the same system. The liver turns THC into another active compound (11-hydroxy-THC), which is stronger and is the main reason edibles hit harder. So differences in your enzymes could change **how strong, how long and how "edgy" or "calm"** THC feels to you.
- **CBD** goes further: it **slows down** some of these same enzymes. Taking CBD can therefore **raise the levels** of other medicines, including some mood stabilisers and seizure medicines. That's one real reason cannabis and "head meds" can clash.

**Your pattern** fits this picture:
- seizures lasting months after a two-day course;
- a sedative acting like a stimulant;
- cannabis acting "backwards".

**That doesn't prove which enzyme is involved.** But it's exactly the kind of history that **pharmacogenomic testing** is designed to explain (§4).

### 3b. Seizure threshold

- Some medications **lower the seizure threshold**: they make the brain more likely to have a seizure, especially at high blood levels.
- If your body clears such a drug slowly (3a), the risk climbs.
- Cannabis is complicated here. **CBD** is an approved anti-seizure treatment for some rare epilepsies. **THC's** effect on seizures is mixed and depends on the dose.
- Practical point: a history of drug-triggered seizures is something to **keep on record** with any doctor, dentist or pharmacist, so it doesn't happen again.

### 3c. "Paradoxical" reactions (getting the opposite effect)

- Medicine has a name for this: a **paradoxical reaction**. A minority of people get **agitated or wired** from drugs meant to calm them, or calm from stimulants.
- It's documented for several drug classes, not just one. Suggested reasons include individual brain chemistry, age and genetics.
- Being **"backwards" on sedatives and on cannabis** at the same time is consistent with a nervous system that responds atypically in general. In other words, **you're not imagining it, and you're not alone**.

### 3d. Why "indica sedates, sativa energises" fails for you (and for many people)

- **The labels themselves are unreliable.** Lab studies of commercial cannabis have found that the indica/sativa names **poorly predict** what's actually in the product. Products with the two labels often have **overlapping chemistry**.
- What seems to matter more:
  - the **THC : CBD balance**;
  - the **terpenes** (aroma compounds such as myrcene, limonene and pinene), which may shape the feel;
  - the **dose**.
- **THC is "biphasic".** Low doses are often calming, while higher doses can cause anxiety or racing. So "this strain is speedy" might partly be "this strain is stronger for me".
- **People's cannabinoid systems differ too.** There is inherited variation in the body's own cannabinoid receptors and in the enzyme that clears the body's own "bliss molecule" (anandamide). That's an active research area: plausible, not settled.

**Put together:** your "opposite effect" is most likely a mix of **your own biology** (3a–3c) and **labels that don't describe the chemistry** (3d). That's exactly why your ten-year self-tracking method works: you learned **your response to specific products**, not what the label promised.

### 3e. Cannabis and mood stabilisers or other "head meds"

Even for people who *can* take psychiatric medications, cannabis can matter in two ways:
1. **Chemical interactions** (3a): CBD can raise the levels of some of these medicines, and some medicines can change THC levels.
2. **Effects on mood itself:** heavy, high-THC use is linked in research with worse outcomes for some mood and psychotic conditions in some people. That's a reason to keep tracking honestly, which you already do.

Your situation, where those medications themselves cause adverse reactions, is a decision for you and a clinician who listens. **It isn't something a label, an employer or an AI can settle for you.**

## 4. Things that could actually help you

1. **Ask about a pharmacogenomic (PGx) test.**
   - It's a cheek swab or blood test that reports which versions of the main drug-processing enzymes you carry.
   - It can turn "I react badly to meds" into a **documented medical fact**.
   - That may help with:
     - your doctors (safer choices);
     - your **disability application** (objective evidence of your treatment limits);
     - explaining to others why cannabis is your option.
   - Some are ordered by a doctor; coverage varies.
2. **Get your reactions written into your medical record as adverse drug reactions** (with dates), not just "doesn't like". Pharmacists then get an automatic alert.
3. **Keep your journal going.** Product, tested THC/CBD/terpenes, amount, time, effect at 30 minutes and at 2 hours. It's the same template as in Lucien's proposal, and it's evidence.
4. **Look into a medical cannabis card** if your state has one. It puts your use on an official medical footing.
5. **Work and teaching:** the rules on cannabis and employment differ a lot between states and employers. I can't give legal advice. A **disability advocate** or legal aid office near you can tell you what protections apply.
