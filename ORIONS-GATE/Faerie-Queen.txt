Faerie Queen

ACT ONE: IN THE VALLEY 
1. The Path That Ate History
Thomas came into the Sibundoy Valley in a jeep that had lost its shock absorbers years before it lost its paint. The road from Pasto clung to the mountain like a nervous animal. On one side the earth dropped away in green shelves toward Putumayo, so steep that the fields looked pasted on with spit. On the other side, cloud tore itself on cold ridges and put itself back together as rain. The air smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, diesel, and sugarcane cooked down until it darkened. Every time the jeep hit a stone, Thomas’s teeth clicked and the notebook in his coat jumped like a small guilty animal.
He had brought the notebook as a shield. He believed, without admitting it, that if he could write a place down he would not have to be changed by it. By the time they descended into the wide bowl of the valley, the shield already felt foolish. The valley floor was pasture and small fields and horses standing in fog up to their knees. Wooden houses sat under low tile roofs. Women in bright skirts moved between stalls of eggs, herbs, plastic bowls, and fried things wrapped in paper. From the far end of town a church bell made a thin sound that did not fill the mountains. The mountains did not notice the bell.
The driver stopped without ceremony. Thomas paid him and stood in the street with his pack, waiting for the place to arrange itself into a scene he could understand. It refused. A dog slept in a puddle as if the puddle were a plan. A radio in a doorway argued about football. A boy in rubber boots looked at Thomas once, the way a person looks at weather, then pointed with his lips toward a track that left the last houses and entered trees. Thomas said thank you in Spanish that sounded like it had been ironed. The boy was already gone.
He walked. The track began as mud and became root. Cloud moved through the eucalyptus at the edge of town and then through trees that belonged here, trees with bark like burned paper and leaves that dripped even when the rain had paused. He could hear water without seeing it. He could smell pig and wood and a cold sweetness he could not name. The notebook bumped his ribs. He did not take it out. For the first time in months he had the unpleasant sense that writing would be a way of not arriving.
2. The Man Who Looked Like Work
Taita Jacinto was scraping mud off a board with a machete. The house behind him seemed to have grown out of the slope rather than been built on it. Smoke leaked from a kitchen roof. A hog watched from a pen with philosophical contempt. Chickens negotiated the yard as if they owned the title. Taita Jacinto was Inga, compact, dark, perhaps sixty, hat stained at the brim, hands mapped by old cuts. When he looked up, the look traveled through Thomas’s coat and stopped on the rectangular bulge of the notebook.
“You wrote that you needed to see plants,” Taita Jacinto said in Spanish first, then in a slow English he had learned for men who arrived with coats and convictions. “Everyone who is lost says they need to see something. Sit there. Not on the chair with the good leg.”
Thomas sat on a crate. The good chair remained empty and somehow louder for being empty. From the kitchen doorway an old woman watched him without curiosity and without welcome. She was darker than the Inga men in the yard, her headwrap the color of wet charcoal, her fingers black at the seams from work. She shelled beans with a patience that made Thomas feel suddenly theatrical.
“Mama Iyabo,” Taita Jacinto said, tipping his chin. “She is not from this valley. Do not make her into your second souvenir. If she speaks, you listen with the part of you that still knows how to be afraid in a useful way.” Mama Iyabo cracked a bean. The sound was small and final. She said nothing.
Thomas cleared his throat. “I can work.”
“That sentence is cheap,” Taita Jacinto said, and went back to the board. “We will find out if the body agrees with the mouth. First you will greet the mountains. Here they are not decoration. They keep this valley from becoming a rumor. If you start with me, you will stay small. Come.”
3. Three Leaves and the Hidden Line of Rock
They walked to a stone at the edge of the chagra. The planted ground was not pretty. It was used: maize, herbs, a few fruit trees leaning as if tired of standing, tobacco in a ragged line. The peaks were hidden in cloud, but the cloud itself had weight, as if the mountains were thinking through water. Wind moved the fog in slow muscles. Somewhere a horse blew air through its lips.
Taita Jacinto took three coca leaves from a small bag, arranged them like a narrow green bird, lifted them toward the hidden line of rock, and blew a breath that had no theater in it. He placed the leaves under a pebble. Then he waited, not for magic, for Thomas.
Thomas copied the gesture and felt, to his irritation, that his hands were the hands of a man performing an imitation for an invisible camera. The leaves trembled. He blew. He set them down too carefully, like a person placing a lie on a table and hoping it would look like bread.
Taita Jacinto smiled with only one side of his mouth. “The mountain does not need your sincerity. It needs you to stop advertising. You blew as if someone might give you a mark.”
“I don’t know the words,” Thomas said.
“Good. Words are how city men hide. The breath was enough if it was only breath. Yours still had a speech inside it. We will try again tomorrow, when you are more tired and less photographed by yourself.”
They walked back. Thomas wanted to ask whether the offering had been accepted. The question felt like asking a river for a receipt. He kept it. Mama Iyabo, still in the doorway, laughed once, very softly, as if a child had finally stopped stirring soup with a book.
4. The Hog and the Private Courtroom
In the afternoon Taita Jacinto fed scraps to the hog and let Thomas talk, which was not kindness so much as diagnosis. Thomas told it badly at first, then more plainly, because the hog’s indifference made style look sick.
A year earlier, behind a rented house in another country, he had pinched a young shoot from a tree. He had felt, or thought he felt, a bright wrongness in the air. He boiled the shoot. He called the pot a little hell. Then he called himself ridiculous. Then he called himself sensitive. He read pages. He stopped eating certain leaves and then ate them anyway and kept a secret ledger of guilt. None of those names had returned the world to a size he could live in. So he had bought tickets until the tickets delivered him to mud.
Taita Jacinto scratched the hog behind the ear. The hog accepted this as its due. “You made the plant into a mirror and then argued with your face. That is a city skill. Here a plant is a person of another order. It does not keep a novel of suffering. It keeps a weather of flow. When you rip the rising current, the field knots. When you take what is already leaving, the field adjusts.”
He looked at Thomas without comfort. “Your problem is not that you felt something. Your problem is that you wanted the feeling to make you special or innocent. Special and innocent men are dangerous in a kitchen and worse in a forest.”
“I can drop that,” Thomas said.
“No. You can watch it. If you say you have dropped it, it only puts on a cleaner shirt.” Taita Jacinto wiped his hands on his trousers. “Tonight you eat. Tomorrow you carry water. The yagé is not a taxi for your guilt.”
Thomas felt heat in his face. “I didn’t come for a taxi.”
“You came for a verdict,” Taita Jacinto said. “Guilty or forgiven. The forest does not run a court. It runs a household. In a household you either belong to the eating or you starve on principles. We will see which one you are.”
5. The Gourd That Held His Hands
Mama Iyabo set soup in his hands and held the gourd an extra second so he had to feel the strength in her fingers. “Eat,” she said. Her Spanish carried another coast inside it, a dryness under the Andean wet. “A starved head manufactures spirits and then weeps when they will not hire it.”
The soup was potato, smoke, a green herb that cut the fat of the broth, and a heat that made his eyes water. He ate on a low stool while rain began again, so fine it seemed the air itself had started to sweat. A moth chose his wrist and he let it. Gratitude arrived without costume. He disliked how simple it was. He had wanted the first evening to be an initiation. It was salt, potato, and being seen through.
Taita Jacinto watched the gratitude appear and nodded as a man nods when a fish finally moves the line. “There. That is cleaner than your courtroom. Do not decorate it.”
After eating they sat under the kitchen roof. The fire painted the old woman’s face in copper and shadow. She studied Thomas as one studies a tool that might still be useful if the handle is repaired.
“You have the mouth of a man who has been careful and starving at the same time,” she said. “Careful men want innocence. Starving men want a feast of meaning. Both will make a mess of a leaf. Tomorrow you work until the mouth is quieter.”
Thomas slept on a pallet that smelled of wool and smoke. The notebook stayed in the coat. Once he reached for it in the dark and then stopped, because the reaching felt like packing a shirt. Rain worked the roof. The hog dreamed and stamped. On his left side, not as a vision but as a pressure, sat the fact that his body could end on a path tomorrow and the valley would continue its weather. The pressure was strangely friendly. He did not name it. Naming would have been the clerk again.
6. Water, Blisters, and the Costume of Power
They did not drink medicine the next day. They hauled water from a spring that came out of black roots and tasted of stone. The path was slick. Orchids held beads of cold. A hummingbird worked a hanging red flower with a hunger so clean it made Thomas’s complicated appetites look like paperwork. Each jar reset his tongue. By the third trip his shoulders understood they were no longer ornaments.
They cut fodder for guinea pigs whose lives were practical and unashamed. They turned a compost heap that steamed like a buried animal. The valley weather changed every twenty minutes: sun hard enough to bleach the fence posts, then cloud, then rain, then a hard blue hole in the cloud through which a hawk hung as if nailed there.
“A man who wants power and cannot carry water is a child with a costume,” Taita Jacinto said, driving a shovel. “You came with a wound about leaves. The wound can be used. The hunger to be chosen cannot. I have no employment for that hunger.”
Thomas’s palms blistered. He wanted to say he had crossed a continent. The sentence stayed in his mouth, which was the first intelligent thing he had done since the jeep. Mama Iyabo passed with a basin, glanced at the blisters, and did not cluck.
“Good,” she said. “Now the body is joining the conversation. Last night only the head was applying for work.”
At dusk Thomas sat on the crate again and looked at the chagra until the plants stopped being scenery. Tobacco stood in a ragged line like uncles who had stopped pretending to be ornamental. A young fruit tree leaned toward the wash-water. The stone where they had offered coca looked like a stone. He almost said so aloud, then understood that saying it would be a way of asking the stone to perform.
Taita Jacinto handed him a banana as if holiness were comic and edible. “Tomorrow we go down into the eyebrow. If you talk on the narrow path I will send you back for jars.”
7. Where the Andes Begin to Spill
On the third morning they walked downslope from the valley toward the forest that is neither highland nor jungle, the wet shoulder where cold water becomes green appetite. The change was metabolic. Pasture gave way to dripping ferns. Bromeliads held rain in pale cups. A cock-of-the-rock crossed a ravine like a thrown coal. The smell thickened into rot, nectar, leaf-mold, and cold iron.
Thomas’s eyes kept trying to frame it. The forest refused. It was too particular. A spiderweb held a hundred suns of water. A log wore orange fungi like a quiet mouth. The creek spoke in several throats at once. Something unseen moved and the undergrowth remembered how to be a body. He felt his city walk, the walk of a man crossing a park, and tried to put it down. The attempt itself was still city. He breathed and let the feet be less important.
Taita Jacinto paused where the path pinched between two stones and touched the earth with two fingers. “Here the land narrows. A man who talks through a narrow place falls and then writes about destiny.”
They passed in silence. On the other side Mama Iyabo stopped by a stand of cane and turned her skull slightly, listening with bone. “Old death in this water,” she said, as one might mention salt. “Not yours. Do not pocket it. Thin men collect other people’s dead because their own life feels light.”
Thomas left the question unasked. Taita Jacinto noticed the leaving and did not praise him. Praise would have restarted the special person. A bird called a note that seemed to stitch two slopes together. Thomas did not make the note into a message. Leaving it as bird was already work.
8. The Portrait at the Instant of Taking
They came to a shrub with small tough leaves. Behind it a vine did private calligraphy on the slope. Light moved in the wet as if it had bones. Taita Jacinto squatted and looked at Thomas, not at the plant.
“Take one leaf. Not because we need it. Because I want the portrait of your spirit at the instant of taking. Most portraits are ugly. The face becomes a customer.”
The old courtroom rose on schedule. Taita Jacinto shook his head. “Do not prosecute. Ask with the chest. If the chest closes, you walk. If it opens, you cut clean and you do not baptize the cut with a speech about your goodness. The plant already knows whether you are good. It is interested in whether you are here.”
Thomas waited until the performance tired. It took longer than he wanted. The shrub condensed. Not a cartoon face. A density, as if one body were a mouth for a larger pattern. He asked, awkwardly, inside, without a script. The chest made a small room. One shining tip seemed to flare, too bright, a sentence still being spoken into light. He left it. He cut a mature leaf.
The sound was indecently intimate. A brightness flared in the twig and went. No scream. A gasp in the field, then a sealing. Thomas stood holding the leaf as if it could bruise further and then, catching himself, held it like a thing already offered.
Mama Iyabo said, “You did not suck it with your eyes. That is a kind of eating worse than teeth.”
Taita Jacinto set the leaf on a stone in moving air. “Watch it become quieter. That quiet is not the hell you invented in your kitchen. The current is going home. Later fire will be another home. You have been confusing change with cruelty because you have never kept manners through either.”
They sat. Water left the leaf by degrees. The green shout thinned. Thomas felt, without being given a proverb, the difference between a young tip and an old blade: one a rising will, the other a traveler already packing. His year of opera shrank to a simple fact. Interruption has degrees. Relatives do not strip a house to prove they can feel.
“I wanted them to scream so I could stay at the center,” he said, surprising himself.
Taita Jacinto’s eyes warmed by a millimeter. “There. That is seeing. Not lights. Proportion. Do not make a shrine of it. Carry it like a knife that is for work.”
9. Coca, the Hawk, and the President in the Head
That afternoon they sat under an overhang where the rock sweated. Taita Jacinto gave him coca and watched whether he imitated like a monkey or entered like a person. Thomas arranged three leaves badly, then better, and blew toward the hidden peaks because the peaks were there. The bitterness opened a thin wakefulness behind the eyes, old as fields.
“A seer learns to stop the world,” Taita Jacinto said, almost idle. “Students hear this and wait for angels. It means the inner clerk loses his job. That clerk thinks he is president. While he shouts, the mountain can stand on your foot and you will call it mood.”
He tapped Thomas’s sternum hard enough to be remembered. “Inventory. Not the costume. The animal.”
Thomas looked inward and disliked the furniture. The rented rooms. The leaving before a place could claim him. The fear of being ordinary braided with the fear of being a monster. The religion he had made of plant-pain so he would not have to become a relative. The tickets bought so his life would look chosen.
He said it. Mama Iyabo slapped her knee once. “Now the stomach can receive something. Lies cannot digest medicine. They vomit it as beauty and go home wearing the same shirt.”
A hawk crossed the white lid of cloud. Thomas watched until watching had no caption. For a few seconds there was only wing and cold. Then the clerk rushed back with an essay about signs and teachers and whether he was doing it right. Taita Jacinto laughed through green teeth.
“You almost stopped it. Then you published the hawk. The hawk does not subscribe.”
Thomas laughed too, short and clean, and felt the pressure on his left side adjust as if pleased to be included in honest company. He did not ask if that pressure was death. Asking would have been publishing again.
10. Water on the Skull
Night made the kitchen a cave. Mama Iyabo heated water with leaves he could not name and washed his head without requesting a permit from the ego. The water smelled of smoke and citrus. Her thumbs found the base of his skull and pressed until a grief with no immediate plot rose and left through his face. He did not decorate the grief. That restraint felt like the beginning of adulthood.
“In my mothers’ country a plant has a mother,” she said, working. “You do not snatch the child on the road because your heart is noisy. You greet the mother. You state hunger like a grown person. You leave a true thing. Breath. Water. A coin. A fact. If you leave a lie, the mother follows you and sits on your chest until the lie is paid.”
“Which mother did I insult?”
“The mother of usefulness. You wanted to be innocent more than you wanted to belong to eaters. Trees are not innocent. Cats are not innocent. Innocence is sold to people who do not want hands.”
She turned his face toward the fire. Her eyes were milky at the rims and exact at the centers. “You will drink his yagé because it opens the stitch your schools sewed through the seeing muscle. I will sit with tobacco and a road that is not Inga. Two laws. One body. Collect us like stamps and the night will break you for exercise.”
Taita Jacinto mended a sandal in the corner. “She likes you. If not, she would have left your pretty head dirty so the medicine could smell you coming.”
Thomas said, “I don’t want to collect you.”
Mama Iyabo’s mouth tipped. “Wanting is perfume. We will watch what you do when you are tired.”
11. The Diet That Is a Battle
The days before the cup were stripped. No salt. No spice. No coffee. No confession as entertainment. Work at first light. Rest when the sun stood on the ridge. Coca with purpose. Thomas’s body campaigned like a deposed official. His dreams came in hard colors: a supermarket flooding with roots; a childhood table where silence sat in the fourth chair; Thomas cutting a tree that bled receipts.
Taita Jacinto used the dreams as tools. “Receipts are your people. You think taking is paperwork. The tree is trying to return you to exchange.”
Mama Iyabo said, “When you wake, put feet to floor before you make meaning. Meaning is a fast animal. Catch it after you have bones.”
Twice Thomas packed a shirt. Twice fog closed the path toward town with a thoroughness that made coincidence look lazy. Taita Jacinto only said, “The mountain has humor. Worse than mine.”
On the fourth evening Thomas sat by the fence and the yard plants ceased to be background. Not vision. Proximity. Tobacco regarded him. The drying leaf on the stone had become a thinner presence, concentrated, listening from farther away. He did not announce this. Announcement would have turned it into capital.
Taita Jacinto nodded once, as if a fence staple had finally seated. “Less noise. Now the vine can risk you. It is not a ladder. It is a mirror that bites. Tonight you may still walk down. If you stay, you stay as a relative, not as a customer of lights.”
Thomas looked at the packed shirt in his mind and asked it to sit. “If I go down I will buy kind food and call it healing because I thought about intention.”
“Then stay,” Mama Iyabo said. “Staying is the first magic that costs.”
12. The House Swept for Weather
They swept the earth floor of the ceremonial house until dust rose and settled into honesty. Bucket. Water. Blankets. Mapacho. Through the open wall the stars were sharp enough to feel personal. A frost-edge lived in the wind even while the forest below kept its wet breath. A dog barked far away and another dog answered, both of them believing in borders with a faith humans reserve for ideas.
Taita Jacinto uncovered the pot. The smell came up dark, root-bitter, like burned leaf and coin and river-iron. He did not make a speech for a future memoir. He named the mountains as neighbors. He named the ground as a being that could withdraw consent. He named the vine and the leaf as persons who had already been asked.
Then he looked at Thomas. “Do not ask to be shattered as a hobby. Ask for a working eye. If you want fireworks, go down to town and spend money.”
“I want to know how to live with them without becoming a thief or a statue.”
Mama Iyabo grunted. “That is food. The other prayers are perfume.”
She smoked a road that was not Andean into the corners. Taita Jacinto drank first, grimacing like a man, not a poster. “Always watch whether the guide enters the same weather. Not for holiness. For company.”
He filled a small gourd. Thomas drank. The bitterness colonized the face. The body considered rejection, then accepted the job. Heat opened in the belly. The beams of the roof became more themselves. In the yard the tobacco plants were suddenly present without being seen, a council that had been waiting for human noise to fall below a certain level.
13. Proportion, Not Jaguars
Time lost its civil service. The first motion was not jaguar gods. It was proportion. The tree of last year’s private trial arrived not as a prosecutor but as a specific life Thomas had handled like a supply. He whispered an apology that had no audience in it. A warmth moved through the sternum, occupational rather than affectionate.
“They are not screaming,” he said.
Taita Jacinto’s leaf-rattle answered first, dry seeds agreeing. “What then.”
“They are crowded. I walked through a city of persons calling it salad.”
“Keep the proportion. If you make them into tortured children you will only crown yourself again.”
The purge came with no poetry. Knees in dirt. The bucket ringing. Mama Iyabo’s hand on his back was a fact. “There goes the fried road food and the sentence I am fine. Spit. Do not be elegant.”
He spat and laughed at the floor. The laugh opened another inch. The brew-person stood in the blood as a braided exactness, neither kind nor cruel. Growing tips flared in memory as bright threads. Mature leaves dimmed like embers allowed to finish. The drying leaf on the stone was a sunset in miniature. The old boiling-pot horror stood up, found no chair, and sat back down. Fire was not hell. Fire was a priest if the taking had been clean.
Taita Jacinto sang a short song that arranged the air without explaining itself. Mama Iyabo answered with a low line from another ocean. The two roads did not merge into a souvenir. They made a tension Thomas could sit inside. In that tension the chest learned a law without receiving a pamphlet: take little, take ripe, speak, complete the use, protect the stand, return a true thing.
He said it aloud to pin it. Taita Jacinto murmured, “Enough law. If you carry more you will sell it in the morning.”
Pictures came and were not clung to. A man waiting on a stem to see if growth would forgive. A forester knee-deep in cold water under a stripped slope. A pale thinking under the soil that knew the weight of a foot. Thomas let them pass. What remained was the garden, densely alive, no longer empty, no longer a courtroom.
Near the low point of the night the old self arrived wearing boots, offering brilliance: become the one who sees, the one excused from dishes by vision. Taita Jacinto’s voice cut through, ordinary as wire. “Choose.”
Thomas put the night down. “I will carry water in the morning.”
Mama Iyabo laughed, low and pleased. “There. A small death. Real. The big one later, if you keep your word.”
14. Banana, Jars, and the Working Eye
Gray entered like a shy animal. A rooster tried morning, failed, tried again. Thomas sat up too fast, sat back, and found the world still eatable. That was the shock. The assignment had not been to become air. It had been to remain a mouth and a pair of hands inside an economy of living persons.
Taita Jacinto wrapped the rattle. His face was tired and ordinary and therefore trustworthy. “Act one is manners. Anyone can see lights. Few can cut a leaf tomorrow without becoming a clerk or a priest.”
Mama Iyabo tossed him a banana. “Feet on the floor. Then food. Then sleep. If you dream scissors, good. The hand is growing a conscience.”
Thomas peeled the banana. It was ridiculous and sufficient. Out in the yard the tobacco had begun to look like tobacco again, which was not a loss of sight but its ripening. He greeted the hidden peaks with breath, no theater.
“I can live,” he said.
“That was the work,” Taita Jacinto answered. “Next we go farther, not higher. Farther is more accurate. Higher is a drug the clerk enjoys. If you come to shine, I will give you the latrine. If you come to haul water, I will treat you as a person who lives here.”
Thomas slept in the forenoon with dirt on his cheek. The notebook stayed shut. On his left side the friendly pressure remained, a witness against waste. The valley held. The forest below held. He had entered a household and had not yet been asked to speak at the table. For one morning, that was enough seeing.

ACT TWO: LITTLE PEOPLE MAKE ROOM
______
1. The Morning the Clerk Came Back with Papers
Thomas woke into wood, frost-edge, and the smell of last night’s smoke clinging to his hair. The ceremonial house was only a house. The bucket was only tin. For an hour this felt like theft. The clerk in his head, unemployed since the yagé had stopped the world’s chatter, rushed back into office with stamped documents. Nothing happened. You purged. Altitude. Two old people. Suggestion. Go down to Pasto and eat carefully and call it wisdom.
Taita Jacinto was already among the guinea pigs, as if the unseen were a luxury animals could not chew. Mama Iyabo roasted maize and did not ask for a report. When Thomas opened his mouth to manufacture meaning, she pointed at the jars.
“Carry,” she said. “If the eye cannot survive water, it was perfume.”
The spring path was slick with last night’s cloud. Orchids held cold beads. A hummingbird worked a hanging flower with a hunger so clean it shamed every appetite Thomas had ever called love. Each jar reset his tongue. By the third trip the clerk had less breath. What remained of the night was not spectacle. It was proportion. Tobacco in the yard stood as kinship. The dried leaf on the stone had withdrawn into settled usefulness.
Taita Jacinto drank from a jar and wiped his mouth with a working hand. “Good. You did not found a church at breakfast. Last night opened an eye. Today we train the hand. An open eye on a thief’s wrist is how seers become pests.”
Thomas almost said he was not a thief. The sentence died, and that death was the first honest work of the day.
“There is another country in this mountain,” Taita Jacinto said, as if mentioning weather. “The little people. Faerie, if you need an old word from the cold islands. They are not toys and they are not your healing brand. They come when a man has kept a small word with a leaf. They do not come to decorate an unlived life. If they look at you today, do not perform orphan.”
Mama Iyabo snorted. “And do not eat at their table. Reborn men who eat too early become rumors.”
2. Muña and the Face That Must Not Become a Customer
They climbed toward a stand of muña and a young queñua whose bark peeled like sunburned parchment. The air thinned until each inhale was a decision. Far up, a condor was a dark hinge in white. Lichens mapped the stones in slow green script. The valley dropped away into terraces and then into the wet eyebrow of forest.
Taita Jacinto stopped in the muña. The plants lived in a small republic of mint-cold breath. “You take for tea we will drink before sundown. That closes the road. Insight left to rot in a bag is a second wound. The green world files waste under your true name.”
Thomas stepped in and let the chest consult. One plant leaned away, a private closed door. He passed it. Another made a room. He cut two mature sprigs, left the leaders, and did not baptize the act with virtue. The snap was intimate. A tightness flared and passed like weather.
Mama Iyabo poured a drop of water at the root. “Not payment. Payment is how you live after. This is only not kicking the door.”
They found older leaves yellowing on a lower bush. Taita Jacinto had him hold a bright tip without cutting it. The tip was upward will, a sentence still being spoken into light. Then an older leaf, already packing its current. Lifting that one felt like taking a bag a traveler had set down. Thomas left the tip. Last year’s opera about screaming shoots lost its theater and became a fact. Interruption has degrees.
“You want a rule,” Taita Jacinto said, watching his face. “The rule is this portrait. If your face at the cut is a customer, walk away. If your face is a relative, cut clean. Relatives do not strip the house.”
Thomas carried the sprigs in cloth. That small dignity changed the walk home. The hidden peaks did not congratulate. They remained walkable.
3. Shade Road and Fire Road
Half the muña went on a rack in moving shade. Half waited for the pot. Thomas sat near the rack and watched shout become whisper. Water left. Perfume narrowed into itself. The current withdrew like a tide that trusted the moon. No courtroom. A completion.
Mama Iyabo turned a sprig. “Sun finishes some beings. This one wants the cool road. Dry a shade-person in glare and you will drink a bitterness you earned with stupidity, then name it medicine.”
At dusk Taita Jacinto set fresh sprigs in water and let fire do what fire has always done. Steam rose like a second plant. Thomas’s old pot-horror stood up, found no chair, and sat back down. The taking had been clean. The use was near. Transformation was not torture. It was a priest if the first cut had manners.
They drank the bright tea first. It slapped the mouth, green will still close to stem. Later a pinch of yesterday’s stone-dried leaf made a lower cup, a night voice.
“They are not the same person in the mouth,” Thomas said.
“Nothing alive is the same person after a road,” Taita Jacinto said. “You wanted one verdict, cruel or kind. The old way is adult. There is grabbing and there is accompanying. You accompanied. That is why the fire did not accuse you.”
Mama Iyabo tapped his wrist. “Remember when you try to live on air. Air-eaters still wear hides and sleep on cotton. Be a clean eater. The field can use that.”
A wind moved through the kitchen that smelled faintly of crushed apple and cold metal, then became only woodsmoke. Thomas looked at the doorway. No one stood there. Taita Jacinto did not look.
“They inspect the manners before they inspect the man,” he said. “Do not hunt that smell. Hunting is how the little people turn a student into a story they tell each other in winter.”
4. The Child Who Packs Shirts
Afternoon brought the real enemy, which was not a demon in feathers. It was Thomas’s talent for escape. The fence looked like a fence. The peaks looked like rock. Doubt arrived dressed as intelligence. You can go down and buy kind food and remain unclaimed. You can keep a little poetry and no obligation.
He sat by the hog pen. The hog breathed grain and heat through the wire, unimpressed by metaphysics. Taita Jacinto arrived with mapacho and did not ask what was wrong.
“A man aims doubt at the theater, not at the world,” he said. “You want to murder the seeing so your hands can stay old. That is not skepticism. That is homesickness for being unaccountable.”
“What if I invented it.”
“Then invent usefulness equally well. If your invention makes you less leaky, keep it. If it makes you special, compost it. I am not hired to convince you. Conviction is a sweet drink. I am hired to make you available.”
Mama Iyabo bound his blistered palm too tightly on purpose. “Your old self is not a devil. It is a child who learned to leave rooms before they became real. Thank it. Do not give it the mule.”
Thomas put a hand on the packed shirt in the mind and asked it to stay for a second night. The pressure on his left side adjusted its weight. Whether that was spirit or cold hardly mattered. The staying was the magic that cost.
“If the little people come,” Mama Iyabo said, “the child will want to go with them because leaving feels like home. You will tell the child it may sit. It may not drive

5. Coca and the Line in the Dust
Under the sweating overhang they chewed coca toward hidden summits. The bitterness opened a thin corridor behind the eyes. A hawk crossed cloud. This time Thomas did not publish the hawk. For a longer span there was only wing, white, and the body’s small heat. When the clerk returned he was quieter, like a man who has been fired and rehired at lower pay.
Taita Jacinto said, “Last night the vine stopped the world for you. A man who sees must stop a sliver of it sober, or the medicine becomes a crutch with feathers. Ask, if I die on this path before second cup, what in me is still a customer.”
Thomas asked and did not like the answer. The customer wanted a story. The relative wanted a working relationship with leaf, water, fire, and the living poor who still ate. The customer wanted to be innocent. The relative was willing to belong to eaters.
Mama Iyabo drew a line in dust with a stick and had him sit on one side, then the other. “That is all this work is at this stage. Choosing which person drinks. If the customer drinks, you will see palaces and wake cruel. If the relative drinks, you may see only a leaf and wake useful. The little people prefer the relative. They have enough palaces.”
Thomas moved to the relative’s side of the line. The gesture was almost comic. It also held.
“What are they,” he asked. “The little people.”
Taita Jacinto spat green and looked at the cloud as if it were a neighbor with debts. “They are a people. Not a metaphor. Not a feeling in your chest you can sell. They keep roads through places your maps call empty. They keep law about taking, waste, beauty, and bargains. My grandmothers here had names for them in the high grass and in the wet trees. Hers have names from another ocean. The cold islands called them faerie and then made them cute so they would not have to be afraid. Cute is a kind of theft.”
Mama Iyabo nodded. “They help when the eye is already working. They do not open the eye for a lazy man. That is why last night had to happen first.”
6. The Forest Door and the Pale Sewing
They went down into the ceja while light still had bone in it. The eyebrow forest closed around them with a wet intelligence. Leaves dripped as if the air perspired. A trunk wore a ladder of small ferns. The smell was rot and honey and cold iron.
Taita Jacinto stopped at a vine that was not the brew-vine, a lesser cousin doing its own work. He did not cut. He spoke as one speaks to a person in another room, then listened with the skin. Thomas felt an owner-presence behind the local green, the grouped being of a kind. You do not take a teacher-child without greeting the mother. Song was address, not costume.
Mama Iyabo knelt and put her ear near soil. “Under this, a pale thinking. Not a god for your necklace. A web that has been counting since before your cities. When you walk, it knows the weight. When you cut above, it adjusts below. You never harvest one leaf. You touch a city.”
Thomas’s feet felt suddenly loud. Then they learned to be quieter without becoming precious.
On the return they passed a tree scarred by old machete kisses. The tightness in it had eased a hair, or Thomas’s chest had. Taita Jacinto left nothing but breath. “Do not become manager of wounded trees. Repair is local. Guilt is an empire.”
At a place where moss made a sudden velvet saddle, Thomas’s chest closed. His feet turned without debate. Mama Iyabo murmured, “Praise the no.” They praised it by leaving it alone. The air there had that crushed-apple metal smell, stronger. Something small and exact seemed to watch from the height of a fern.
“They marked you a path you will not walk yet,” Taita Jacinto said. “Good. A man who cannot take no from the little people will not take no from a plant either, and then he becomes a problem in every world he enters.”
7. Two Roads, One Backbone
Night kitchen. Mama Iyabo heated water and washed Thomas’s head again, less as ceremony than as husbandry. Citrus, smoke, thumb-pressure at the skull’s root. Grief rose that belonged to no single memory: rooms left too soon, meals eaten as fuel, a long refusal to be claimed.
“My mothers said a plant can call or refuse,” she murmured. “Refusal is medicine. If everything yields to you, you are not beloved. You are a leak. Tonight if a door shuts, you will praise the shut door.”
She had him state hunger out loud, plain as salt. Food. Sight. A life that could keep its word. No request to be shattered for glamour. Taita Jacinto, mending the same sandal, nodded once.
“Two roads,” he said. “Do not braid them into a product. Let them make a tension you can sit in. Sitting in tension is the work. Resolving it into a slogan is clerk work. If the little people open a third road, you still sit. You do not collect countries.”
Thomas sat in the tension and felt ancestors who were not personal stand at the edge of firelight. They did not speak English. They did not need to. Their regard made his private myth look like one bead on a long string. The hero was not being selected. The hero was being cooked for use.
“I am afraid I will want them more than I want the jars,” he said.
Taita Jacinto’s mouth tipped. “That sentence is already more honest than most prayers. Keep it. Honesty is a song plants and little people still recognize.”
8. The Second Weather
They swept the earth floor. Stars like nails. Cold in the wind, wet forest breathing below. Taita Jacinto uncovered the pot and named mountains, ground, vine, leaf, without inflating his voice. Mama Iyabo smoked a road that was not Inga into the corners. Thomas drank second, after the guide, and the bitterness was familiar now, which made it more serious. Familiarity without manners is how people turn teachers into weekends.
The world did not stop with last night’s surprise. It halted like a tool taken up again. Garden tobacco leaned in. The drying rack was a row of small sunsets. Beneath soil the pale web announced itself as a thinking membrane.
Thomas purged sooner, cleaner. Mama Iyabo said, “Less fried bus. More old self. Good. Spit.”
Taita Jacinto’s rattle made small rain. An icaro arranged the rafters. Thomas saw, not as lecture, a man with a watch waiting on a stem to learn if growth would forgive. Forgiveness looked like current consenting to continue in human company. Then a mountain man furious in cold water, trees as coolers of earth’s blood, a cleared slope as a stopped pulse. Then a quiet European watching one leaf become all organs. Then a woman who heard a species-being speak in clean sentences about how it wished to be grown. Then a man who loved a peanut until it offered food for a people. None of them stayed to be collected. They nodded and went.
“The world has had ears before me,” Thomas said.
“Yes,” Taita Jacinto said. “Do not become a museum guard of ears. Become an ear.”
9. Interior Country
The brew climbed into a quieter room. The chest became an organ of hearing, as if intelligence had been misfiled in the skull for years. A plant did not send sentences. It sent pressure, taste, yes-room, no-turn. Thomas asked the muña of the afternoon how the cutting had been and received not words but a settled absence of grievance. Asked a bright unused tip and felt a warning flare, not drama, simply don’t.
Then the interior world opened without scenery first. It was understanding the way a body understands falling. He felt himself as a small weather moving through larger weathers. The group-soul of tobacco was a dry nation. Muña was a clan of cold perfume. The yagé was a long marriage of two teachers. Under all of it the pale web sewed slope to slope. And through that sewing ran another weaving, finer, older in courtesy, the roads of the little people, faerie-road if the old word must be used, not above the forest and not below it, but in the manners of it.
He understood, without a pamphlet, that the otherworld is not elsewhere like a second town on a map. It is the same world with the clerk unemployed. It is kinship made visible. It is law that beauty without obligation is bait. It is the knowledge that a man can die of wanting to stay where the air is arranged.
“You are leaking toward them,” Mama Iyabo said, very close, ordinary. “Sit in your ribs. If you follow every bright corridor you will not come back with hands.”
Thomas sat in his ribs. The corridors remained. He did not run down them. That not-running was the first interior discipline that felt like adulthood.
10. They Arrive as a Change in Manners
They did not arrive as wings from a children’s book. They arrived as a change in manners. The fog at the open wall acquired faces the way frost acquires ferns, slowly, then all at once. Small attentions first: a pressure at the elbow; a laugh like glass beads to the left; crushed apple and cold metal.
Then persons, if persons could be made of glance and leaf-edge and old obligation. One tall as a spear and thin as drought. One round and mossed at the wrists. Eyes too steady. Clothes that were not clothes so much as decisions about season.
The tall one looked at Taita Jacinto with respect and at Mama Iyabo with recognition, as if Africa and this mountain’s other country had traded cousins when the world was less fenced. Then it looked at Thomas.
“You kept a leaf from rotting in a bag,” it said, using English only as a courtesy. “That is why we bother. Most who come here want our light to decorate an unlived life.”
Thomas did not kneel. Kneeling would have been a product. He stood as a relative stands in a stranger’s kitchen, ready to be useful or to leave. “I don’t want your light as jewelry. I want to finish becoming someone who can take and not insult.”
The moss-wristed one smiled with no warmth and much approval. “Then you will have to see what taking is from the inside of the city, not from the inside of your guilt.”
Taita Jacinto had his palms on his knees, a farmer visiting another law without pretending he owned the customs. “He is on dieta. He does not eat at your table.”
“We did not offer food,” the tall one said, almost amused. “We offered proportion.”
11. The City of Green Persons
They did not take Thomas’s body from the mattress. They took the interior. He remained a man on earth with a bucket near his head, and at the same time he stood in a density of regard so complete that last year’s supermarket became a cartoon.
Leaves were persons. Not human faces pasted on green. Intensities. Growing tips burned like written sentences still climbing. Old leaves hung as completed words. A fruit about to fall felt like relief, a milking, a household completing an exchange. A snatched tip from his old life flared across a web of kin as a gasp, not a courtroom scream. A forgotten bag of greens in a city sink stained a taker’s luck the way a broken promise stains a family.
“You wanted screaming so you could remain the sensitive one,” said the moss-wristed folk. “Screaming would have kept you at the center. They are a city. You are a visitor with scissors. Act accordingly.”
Thomas said, “I will.” The words were tested by a dozen unhuman hearings. Some of the words fell off. What remained was smaller and livable.
The tall one touched nothing and still the heart’s ear opened past what the first night had allowed. Thomas heard, if hearing is the word, how waste is a second cut, how gratitude is not a speech but a completed use, how a stand that cannot continue after your hands is a stand you have robbed. He understood fire as priesthood when the first asking was clean. He understood drying as a grandmother road. He understood that the little people enforce courtesy the way gravity enforces falling: not morally, inevitably.
“Why help me,” he asked.
The tall one tilted its head. “We do not help your personality. We keep the roads clean. A leaky seer dirties more than himself. Your teachers cooked you enough that you are no longer only a leak. That is rare. Rarity interests us. Do not become proud of being interesting.”
Mama Iyabo’s pipe coal glowed in the earth-world that still held his body. “He hears you. Do not take him farther tonight. He still has jars in the morning.”
The round one glanced at her with something like fondness. “We know work. We were not born from picture books.”
12. The Customer Tries to Seize the Bright Corridor
Near the low point of the night Thomas’s old self stood up inside the body wearing its best clothes: sensitivity, intelligence, the romance of leaving, the hunger to go with the little people and never haul water again. It offered a bargain. Keep the poetry. Drop the obligation. Beauty is the real country. The valley is only mud.
The tall one watched this without pity. “That one wants to eat at a table we did not set.”
Taita Jacinto’s voice cut through from the shore of the mattress. “Choose.”
Thomas saw the bargain as a bus ticket printed on the skin. The child who packed shirts reached for the bright corridor. Thomas, shaking, put a hand on that child the way Mama Iyabo had put a hand on a gourd.
“You kept me alive in rooms that had no ritual,” he told it, not aloud to the room, aloud in the interior. “Thank you. You cannot lead a life among persons of bark and leaf and little people. You will make them into mirrors. Sit. Eat maize. Stay.”
The child sat. Not vanished. Seated. That was the victory available to humans.
The moss-wristed one nodded. “Small death. Good. The larger dying is another night. If you run toward a queen now you will arrive as a customer with a prettier coat.”
Thomas did not ask what queen. Asking would have been hunting. He let the not-knowing stand like a praised no on a moss saddle.
The little people thinned the way frost thins when sun is only a rumor. Crushed apple faded into woodsmoke. The pale web remained. The garden remained. Thomas remained a man who would be hungry at dawn.
“Remember us as law, not as longing,” the tall one said, already more fog than person. “Longing makes you stupid. Law makes you a relative.”
13. What the Mouth Keeps
Before dawn they gave him a little of the shade-dried muña in warm water. The taste was the afternoon’s taking, completed. He drank and understood, without a proverb, why the old ones bother with slowness. The leaf had been accompanied to a second life. The body received it as kin.
Taita Jacinto wrapped the rattle. “Second night is not higher. It is more accurate. You met mothers, webs, uncles of the leaf, and the little people who keep the inner roads. Fine. Accuracy is this: tomorrow you will cut, dry, boil, eat, and protect without making a shrine of your sensitivity or a joke of theirs.”
Mama Iyabo pressed his crown. “A farther gate is not for tonight. Crowns. The dying that is not metaphor. If you run toward that now you skip your hands. Hands first. The other world loves a person who can still mend fence.”
Thomas lay on earth and felt worms labor under the ear. The valley held. In the ceja the pale web thought its long thought. In the manners of the fog a people had inspected him and not taken him. He did not try to take a message to market. The message was the life that would have to be lived in daylight: jars, blisters, tea, a no accepted as medicine, a yes used completely, longing seated like a child who is no longer allowed the mule.
Gray came. Frost smoked off the thatch. Taita Jacinto pointed at the spring path.
“Carry.”
Thomas carried. At the spring a single mature leaf floated, yellowing, already packing. He left it to the water. The chest stayed open. On the return, tobacco regarded him as one regards a junior who has survived a shift. A wind smelled briefly of cold metal and crushed apple, then became only Andes air. He nodded as one nods to kin who are already walking away.
“I don’t want a postcard,” he said at the fence.
Taita Jacinto put on his hat. “Then rest. Eat. Do not flirt with the farther crown. If it wants you, it will smell that you have hands now.”
Thomas slept in the forenoon with dirt on his cheek. In the last drift there was a gate that was not the unpainted one at the yard, and a standing that was not Taita Jacinto’s, and a regard that would one day ask whether he had died clean enough to be claimed. He did not knock. The mountain, the old woman, the vine, and the little people seemed, for once, to agree that this was enough otherworld for a man who still had water to haul.

ACT THREE: A NAME THAT ANSWERS

1. The Day the Valley Was No Longer Large Enough
Thomas carried jars until his shoulders understood they were no longer ornaments. The spring came out of black roots and tasted of stone. On the third trip a branch heavy with wet moss dripped into the path and he stepped around it without making a story, which Taita Jacinto later called the only miracle worth paying for. Mama Iyabo had him mend a rip in the guinea-pig net with fingers that still wanted to tremble from the second night. The trembling worked. The net held. Usefulness, it turned out, was a kind of prayer that did not need an audience.
By afternoon the Sibundoy bowl looked complete and therefore insufficient. Cloud tore itself on the ridges and sewed itself back into rain. Horses stood in fog up to their knees. The chagra smoked faintly where wet earth met sun. Thomas could greet a leaf now without founding a religion. That should have been the end of a tidy tale. It was not. Something behind the high green kept pressing the sternum, not the vine, not the pale web, a courtesy older and brighter and less interested in being useful to farms.
Taita Jacinto sat on the chair with the good leg at last and pointed Thomas to the crate. “You kept your word two nights. That buys you a threshold, not a crown. Beyond my fence there is a seeing that kills the name you answer to in towns. I can walk you to the door. I will not pretend the door works for me. I work for mountains, vine, and hungry animals. What lives farther works for a different law.”
Mama Iyabo was already packing tobacco, cool water, a strip of red cloth. “My mothers knew that law under other names. The little people. Faerie, if the cold islands must speak. Crossing folk. They steal you if you go as a customer and claim you if you go as a relative who has already died a little. You will not flirt. You will not bargain with beauty. Beauty is how that country tests whether your hands are still hands.”
Thomas said, “If I don’t go I will spend years polishing the second night into a necklace.”
Taita Jacinto’s mouth tipped. “Then we go at dusk. Eat first. The dead travel badly on an empty stomach, and you are going to be dead for a while.”
2. What a True Name Costs Before It Is Spoken
They ate maize and broth. Thomas wanted the meal to feel like a last supper and hated himself for wanting that. Taita Jacinto watched the wanting the way he watched a fence for gaps.
“Listen to me as a man, not as a hallway of wisdom,” he said. “In every country that still remembers, a name is not a label. A name is a handle. If you grab the world by the wrong handle you spill it. The little people know this better than priests. They will ask what you are called. If you give them the town name, they will have you. If you give them a pretty new name you invented to sound finished, they will have you more gently, which is worse. If you give them the word your chest can stand inside, they may let you keep your feet.”
Thomas turned the spoon. “I don’t know that word.”
“Good,” Mama Iyabo said. “Knowing too soon is a leak. You will die first. Then the word arrives, if you have not decorated the dying.”
Wind moved the kitchen smoke. For a breath it smelled of crushed apple and cold metal. Taita Jacinto did not look at the door.
“They are already inspecting the manners of the meal,” he said. “Chew. A man who cannot finish maize has no business asking for a crown.”
Thomas chewed. Each swallow stitched him to the stool, the hog’s distant complaint, the ordinary holiness of saltless food. He understood, not as theory, that wizardry—if the old work must have a tall word—was not lights. It was knowing which name to set down and which to keep in the mouth like a coal.
3. The Path That Forgot to Be a Path
They left the yard when the peaks took bronze. Coca toward the hidden line of rock. Breath toward the ground that could withdraw consent. Mama Iyabo spat a little water at the threshold and told the child-self to stay with the hog if it could not stop packing shirts. Thomas felt the child sit down in the ribcage, sulking, present, no longer driving.
The track rose, then sideways, then into a cloud so thick the world became listening. Ferns combed their legs. A sudden orchard of waxen flowers hung in the fog like lamps someone had forgotten to light. Taita Jacinto stopped talking. Even his boots seemed to ask permission. The forest had a polish, an arranged wildness, as if intelligence had been gardening without tools.
Thomas’s chest consulted the way it had learned among muña. Some places opened. One narrow saddle of moss closed so firmly his feet turned without debate. Mama Iyabo murmured, “Praise the no,” and they praised it by leaving it alone.
A wind without weather moved the leaves in a single direction. Taita Jacinto halted at a standing stone furred with pale lichen. “Here I am guest,” he said, and the admission cost him something Thomas could hear. “If they take you, do not call for me as a father. Call for the word you kept with the leaves. That word is the only passport I trust.”
He set his hat on the stone. Mama Iyabo tied the red cloth at Thomas’s wrist. “If you see a table, do not eat. If you see a dance, do not show off. If you see a queen, do not perform orphan. She can smell theater through a mountain.”
Thomas wanted a joke and found none that would not leak. The three of them sat until breath matched. Then the two old powers began to sing so quietly it might have been only exhaling. The vine was not in a cup this time. It was in the agreement of the night, a thin bitter thread still in the teeth from prior weather. The world began to stop sliver by sliver, until commentary could not find employment.
4. The Little People at the Shore
They arrived as a change in manners. The fog acquired faces the way frost acquires ferns, slowly, then all at once. Small attentions first: a pressure at the elbow turning Thomas from a drop; a laugh like glass beads to the left; crushed apple and cold metal. Then persons made of glance and leaf-edge and old obligation. One tall as a spear and thin as drought. One round and mossed at the wrists. Eyes too steady. Clothes that were decisions about season.
They looked at Taita Jacinto with respect and at Mama Iyabo with recognition, as if Africa and this mountain’s other country had traded cousins when the world was less fenced.
The tall one said to Thomas, using English only as a courtesy, “You kept a leaf from rotting in a bag. You seated the child. You did not hunt us when we were only a smell. That is why the farther gate will open. Most men come loud and leave as stories we tell to frighten our own young.”
Thomas stood as a relative stands in a stranger’s kitchen. “I don’t want your light as jewelry. I want to finish becoming someone who can take and not insult.”
The moss-wristed one smiled with no warmth and much approval. “Then you will be challenged. Not by a monster. By a law that knows your old names better than you do.”
Taita Jacinto had his palms on his knees. “He is still an animal. Do not take the body if you can help it.”
“We are not hungry for meat,” the tall one said. “We are hungry for clean handles. Come, Thomas-who-is-not-yet-here. The Queen does not wait on customers. She waits on weather.”
Mama Iyabo tied nothing more. She only said, “Remember maize.”
5. Interior Country, Without Maps
They did not drag his flesh from the stone. They took the interior. He remained a man in cloud with two elders on a shore, and at the same time he walked a road that was the manners of the forest made visible. The pale web under the soil was braided with faerie-road, fungus-road and little-people-road lovers before anyone invented separate offices. Growing tips burned like written sentences. Old leaves hung as completed words. To step wrongly was to feel a gasp move through a city of kin.
Thomas understood then what the first two nights had only shown in pieces. The otherworld is not a second town. It is this town with the clerk unemployed. It is the true name of taking. It is beauty as bait and beauty as law. It is the knowledge that a man can die of wanting to stay where the air is arranged.
The tall one spoke without moving its mouth in any human way. “Every leaf has a handle. Every mountain. Every hunger. Your kind grabs by the pretty word and spills the thing. Tonight you will be asked for handles until you bleed ordinary.”
They passed a table set with fruit that smelled like childhood and victory. Thomas did not eat. They passed a dance that would have made his leaving-rooms life look like a chosen myth. He did not show off. The moss-wristed one glanced at the red cloth.
“Good. The old woman taught you something that cannot be sold.”
6. The Queen Who Does Not Perform Queen
The ring of mushrooms and pale stones was not a stage. It was a fact, like a cliff. Taita Jacinto and Mama Iyabo stopped at its edge as if a stick had drawn a law in dust. Thomas stepped in.
She was not standing on a dais. She was standing as if she had always been the standing. Height that did not bother to measure itself. A face that would have ruined a painter by refusing to stay one age. Clothes like frost deciding to be cloth. Eyes the color of the spring before it hits the jar. Behind her, barely seen, a gate that was not wood.
Thomas remembered: do not perform orphan. He stood, dirty-kneed, and let the heart’s ear do the speaking.
The Faerie Queen regarded the red cloth, the blister, the seated child, the death still only a pressure on the left shoulder. She regarded the smell of muña, mapacho, guinea pig, and honest vomit that no perfume had covered.
“You may kneel if your knees need it,” she said. The voice was quiet and final, like a mountain choosing to be a voice. “Do not kneel because I am a story you wanted.”
His knees needed it. He knelt as a worker kneels to lift a stone, then stood again because work does not live on the floor.
“I kept some word with the leaves,” he said. “If you take me, take the hands too. I will not be a court jewel.”
Something that might have been humor moved through her face, and then the humor ended.
7. The Challenge That Had No Mercy in It
“Then we begin,” said the Queen. “Not with gifts. With handles.”
The ring tightened. The little people stood as witnesses, not rescuers. Far off, Taita Jacinto’s breath was a shore-sound. Mama Iyabo’s pipe was a coal in another weather.
“Name the leaf you wounded in the other country,” the Queen said.
Thomas reached for mulberry, for species, for the courtroom words. Each one rang false and fell. What remained was not a botanical title. It was the gasp in the field, the rising will cut for sport, the year he had spent making himself the center of that cut.
“I do not own its name,” he said. “I interrupted a sentence. I called the interruption my sensitivity.”
The Queen’s eyes did not soften. “Better. Name what you are.”
He offered Thomas. The town-name hung in the ring like wet paper and tore. He offered student. It tore. He offered seer, traveler, man who had come far. Each pretty handle spilled. The little people did not laugh. They were more frightening for not laughing.
“Again,” said the Queen, and the word was a wind that found every unkept promise in him. “Name the hunger that bought your tickets.”
He wanted to say sight. The true handle was uglier. “I wanted a story that would make my life look chosen. I wanted innocence more than membership among eaters.”
“Name the child.”
“The one who leaves rooms before they become real.”
“Name the hog.”
He almost smiled and did not. “A person of appetite who does not lie about it.”
“Name the pot.”
“A priest if the taking was clean. A mirror of my guilt if it was not.”
“Name me.”
Silence opened. Thomas felt the clerk try to seize queen, faerie, mother, crown, salvation. Each word would have been eating at a table he had been told not to touch. He stood inside the silence until it hurt.
“You are the standing,” he said. “You are the law of courtesy made into a person. I will not put a smaller word on you to keep you.”
The Queen’s regard sharpened until it was almost a blade. “Name the thing you will not give me.”
That was the trap with honey on it. Men fail there by being noble or by being sly. Thomas looked at his blistered palm.
“I will not give you the jars,” he said. “I will not give you Taita Jacinto’s fence or Mama Iyabo’s water on the skull. I will not give you the hog. If adoption means I stop being an animal among animals, I will walk down.”
The little people shifted like grass under wind. The Queen was still for a long time. In that stillness Thomas understood interior country as few men are allowed: that love without a no is only hunger in festival clothes, that a true name is a promise you can stand inside when beauty tries to unmake your hands, that wizardry is the art of not spilling what you hold.
“Again,” she said, and this time the challenge entered him like cold spring.
She showed him the valley dead of drought because men had cut shade-beings into sun-sick servants. She showed him a city of wet leaves in plastic, a thousand small contracts broken by indifference. She showed him himself on a stage explaining plants to dull faces while the stand behind his rented house died of his absence. She showed him a door into her country that would never require blisters.
“Take it,” she said. “Be bright. Be finished. Be excused.”
Thomas’s mouth was dry. The child reached. The seated child, not the driver, but it reached. He put his hand on it.
“No.”
The word was not loud. It did not need to be. It was a handle that fit.
8. The Dying of the Town-Name
“Now,” said the Queen.
There was no kindness in the timing and no cruelty either. The little people stepped back as midwives step back. The name Thomas had used in streets, on tickets, in rented rooms, rose in the throat like a fishbone. The forest waited. The hidden peaks pressed the air.
He tried to keep one souvenir: the cleverness that had survived childhood tables. The souvenir burned. He tried to keep the romance of being a guest forever in other people’s magic. That burned harder. What was left was a body that could carry water and a chest that could take no for an answer.
Dying was not ballet. It was heat in the mouth, a folding of stories, a panic that had no audience left to impress. He went to the knees because the knees were honest. The pale web received the weight. The little people held the edges of him so the body would not follow the name into unmaking.
He saw the old life from outside: unclaimed meals, the pinched shoot, the year of vanity wearing grief. Then that life closed, not as punishment, as a book finished in the weather it deserved.
A gap. Not poetic black. A working dark, the kind roots know. In it he was not a protagonist. He was compost with attention still flickering. That flickering was enough.
9. Here
Coming back hurt in ordinary places. Lungs. Knees. The red cloth at the wrist. He gasped and sounded like a hog finding air, which made Mama Iyabo snort from the shore, a sound like relief refusing to be holy.
The Queen set two fingers on his brow. The seeing flared all the way: green city, pale sewing, mountain blood, ancestor-heat, faerie-road braided through it. Then it settled into a usable brightness, like day after lightning.
“Name,” she said.
He waited for a grand word and received none. What came was a quieter answering, as if the chest itself had become the handle.
“Here,” he said. “I am here.”
The moss-wristed one nodded. “That will do. Titles make leaks.”
The Queen’s voice was almost gentle and therefore more dangerous. “I adopt what has already died clean. Not the shopkeeper. The relative. You will have my regard among my folk. You will still owe the mountains, the vine, the African mothers, the hog, and the next hungry mouth. Adoption is not escape. It is more kin to fail.”
He felt the adoption as a weight that made the chest roomier, not lighter. A name without letters seated itself beside the word here. He did not speak it to own it.
“Go down before you get drunk on belonging,” the Queen said. “Belonging is proven at breakfast. If you return to me as a jewel, I will not know you.”
10. Return Through Three Laws
The ring let him out. Taita Jacinto searched his face the way he searched a fence for gaps. Mama Iyabo took the wrist and felt the pulse as if counting whether the body had followed the name into death.
“Still an animal,” she said. “Good.”
They walked. The fog thinned into ordinary cloud. The arranged wildness became ceja again: rot, honey, iron, dripping fern, a bird stitching slopes. Thomas greeted a mature leaf by not taking it. Greeted a yellowing one by letting it finish. The law was now simply how seeing felt.
At the standing stone Taita Jacinto put on his hat. “You are not my prize. If you come tomorrow to haul water, I will treat you as a person who lives here. If you come to shine, I will give you the latrine.”
“I’ll haul water,” Thomas said, and the Queen’s weight in the chest agreed.
Mama Iyabo walked a little behind, singing almost nothing. “Two roads, one backbone, and now a third country. Do not braid them for sale. Let them argue in you. Argument kept clean is wisdom.”
Dawn reached the yard before they did. The hog complained. Tobacco stood in its dry council. Thomas picked up the shovel without being asked. Blisters opened and did not require meaning. He cut one old leaf for tea, asked, received room, completed the use in the pot at breakfast. No opera. A relative feeding relatives.
Taita Jacinto watched him drink. Something like equality passed between them, not as flattery, as recognition of a man who could now hold a handle without spilling. “The eye is yours,” the taita said. “Do not rent it out.”
Thomas stood tall because the spine had somewhere to put itself. He did not question whether the night had happened. Questioning was the clerk’s old employment. He had fired that clerk. The world remained questionable. He did not.
11. Epilogue: The Man Who Stood in Two Weathers
Years are a kind of weather. They came.
Thomas lived in the valley long enough that his boots learned the spring path without his mind. He hauled water. He mended wire. He took leaf as a relative and refused the bright tip when the chest closed. He dried in shade what wanted shade and gave fire what could bear fire. When men arrived with notebooks, he pointed them at the crate that was not the good chair, and if they talked through the narrow place he sent them back for jars.
The second sight did not flicker on and off like a hired lamp. It stood. He saw the city in a stand of muña and the stand of muña in a city. He heard, when it was time to hear, the group-being of a plant as pressure, taste, room, refusal. He felt the pale web adjust to a cut. He knew when the little people were only a smell and when they were a law at the elbow turning him from a drop. He did not hunt them. Hunting had been burned out of him with the town-name.
Taita Jacinto grew older in the way mountains grow older: by remaining. One evening he put the good chair opposite Thomas and sat on the crate himself. Mama Iyabo saw it and did not comment, which was comment enough.
“You are not me,” Taita Jacinto said. “Do not try to be. You carry three countries without making a product. That is your work. If you ever doubt it, doubt with your hands, not with your mouth.”
Thomas inclined his head. Equality was not sameness. It was standing in the same weather without leaking. “I won’t rent the eye.”
“Then we are two men who can keep a house,” the taita said. “That is all a shaman is, when the feathers are put away.”
On a later dusk the crushed-apple wind moved through the chagra and Thomas stood in it without reaching. Far behind the high green he felt the standing that was the Queen, not calling, only regarding. He regarded back, a relative at breakfast distance, and went to feed the hog.
The hog accepted this as its due. Thomas almost laughed. The laugh did not leak.
If there was wizardry in him now, it had no costume. It was the art of true handles: here for a name, no for a bait, yes for a ripe leaf, water for the living, silence for the hawk, and a spine that remembered how to stand when beauty asked him to be finished. He was not finished. He was present. The valley did not need his story. He told it anyway only when a student arrived hungry enough to carry jars, and even then he told it with his hands first, because the little people were listening, and because the dead man he had been would have published the hawk.
He stood tall in the mud, second-sighted as the old powers who had cooked him, no longer bargaining with the world for a chosen life, and when the church bell made its thin sound that did not fill the mountains, he went on working, which is how a true name keeps from spilling.
